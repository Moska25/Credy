Why this matters more than any chart on this page. Score PSI is the monitor most credit risk functions actually run, because it needs no outcomes — just the score distribution. Here it stayed comfortably in the "stable" band all the way to month {{ d.score[-1].month }}, while the observed default rate rose sharply. The reason is mechanical: the model cannot see the drift, so it keeps producing the same-looking scores. A monitor built only on model outputs is blind to concept drift by construction. Outcome-based monitoring is the only thing that catches it, and outcomes arrive late.

Columns are application months, values are PSI against the pooled training months {{ meta.split_months.train[0] }}–{{ meta.split_months.train[1] }} (which is why the first twelve columns are near zero — they are being compared with themselves). Numeric features use ten reference quantile bins; categoricals use category shares. Missing values are excluded from PSI and tracked separately below, because a rising missing rate and a shifting distribution are different incidents with different fixes.

The 0.10 / 0.25 cut-points are industry convention, not statistics. They have no distributional justification and they do not adjust for sample size: with {{ (meta.rows / meta.months) | round | int }} applications a month, a PSI of 0.05 is indistinguishable from noise, and with a million rows it would not be.

Both are ramps, not step changes, which is why PSI takes months to cross a fixed threshold. A slow drift is the hardest kind to alert on: by the time a conventional threshold trips, the population has already moved a long way.

identical rules, identical cohort sizes, all four drift switches off
A detector that always fires is not a detector. Two rules deserve comment. score_psi never fires on either cohort — it is not producing false alarms, it is simply blind here. auc_floor fires on the stationary control too, because it compares against a fixed 0.70 floor that happens to sit close to this model's genuine out-of-sample level; that is a badly chosen threshold, not evidence of drift, and it is exactly the kind of thing a control run exposes.

This table is the whole argument for synthetic data. Because the generator wrote the drift schedule, each detector can be scored rather than admired. The lags are the interesting part: three of the four drifts are linear ramps, and a ramp has to grow for several months before a fixed threshold on a {{ (meta.rows / meta.months) | round | int }}-application cohort can separate it from noise. On a real portfolio you would never know whether a quiet month meant no drift or an insensitive detector.
